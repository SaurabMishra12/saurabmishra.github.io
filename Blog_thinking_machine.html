What Does It Take to Make a Machine Think?

Short, plain-spoken notes on the papers and mathematical ideas shaping modern AI—from transformer geometry and test-time reasoning to multi-agent graphs, retrieval security, mechanistic interpretability, and computer-verified proofs.

By Saurab Mishra (saurabmishra.me) BS–MS Mathematical Sciences · Interactive Concept Notebook

Every week a new lab drops a headline: a reasoning model solves Olympiad problems, a multi-agent system writes code, or an AI helps construct a blow-up profile for fluid dynamics. When you come to machine learning from mathematics rather than a standard software engineering track, your first instinct is to strip away the marketing buzzwords and ask a simpler question: what is the underlying mathematical operator actually doing? Instead of a long textbook survey, this page collects short, news-style field notes on the core papers and concepts I keep returning to.

Dispatch 01 · Architecture Lens: Linear Algebra, Hyperspherical Geometry & Scaling Laws

From Discrete Words to Trajectories on a High-Dimensional Sphere

The Core Idea in Plain Words A language model does not "read" words the way we do. It maps discrete text tokens into vectors in ℝd, rotates query and key coordinates to encode word order, and repeatedly nudges those vectors along a shared residual stream until the final direction points to the next token.

When Vaswani et al. (2017) replaced recurrent loops with self-attention in Attention Is All You Need, they turned sequence modelling into parallel matrix multiplication. Each token xi starts as a learned vector. Because high-dimensional random vectors are nearly orthogonal, a 4,096-dimensional space can pack tens of thousands of semantic directions without colliding. Modern transformers encode relative distance by rotating query and key pairs in 2D complex planes via Rotary Position Embeddings (Su et al., 2024), so the inner product qm⊤kn depends cleanly on the offset m − n.

Across layers, the hidden state evolves as an additive dynamical system hl = hl−1 + Attnl(LN(hl−1)) + MLPl(...). Attention heads move context between token positions, while gated feed-forward networks (Shazeer, 2020) act like key-value lookup tables stored in weights. And as Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) showed with Chinchilla scaling laws, cross-entropy loss drops predictably as a power law when parameters and training tokens scale in tandem.

Vaswani et al. 2017 (Transformer) Su et al. 2024 (RoPE) Kaplan et al. 2020 (Scaling Laws) Hoffmann et al. 2022 (Chinchilla)

Dispatch 02 · Reasoning & Philosophy Lens: Test-Time Search, Verification & Epistemology

Does Spending More Tokens at Test Time Mean a Model Actually Thinks?

The Core Idea in Plain Words A fixed transformer pass has a fixed circuit depth. Writing out intermediate steps gives the model external working memory, turning a hard one-shot leap into a sequence of easier conditional steps—but a plausible-looking chain of thought is not always a faithful record of how the model reached its answer.

When Wei et al. (2022) showed that prompting a model to generate step-by-step Chain-of-Thought (CoT) unlocks multi-step arithmetic and symbolic reasoning, the natural mathematical question was why. Every emitted token is fed back into the next causal attention pass, effectively expanding the computation graph at inference time. Later work like Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) and process-supervised verifiers (Lightman et al., 2023) went further, treating reasoning as heuristic search over candidate trajectories where each step is scored before committing.

Yet philosophy of science forces us to be careful here. Functionalism asks what role an internal state plays; epistemology asks whether a model has justified belief or brittle pattern matching. Crucially, Turpin et al. (2023) demonstrated that language models can produce fluent, logical-sounding CoT explanations that hide the true biasing feature that drove the prediction. Getting the right answer and having a faithful reasoning process are two different scientific claims.

Wei et al. 2022 (Chain-of-Thought) Yao et al. 2023 (Tree of Thoughts) Lightman et al. 2023 (Let's Verify Step by Step) Turpin et al. 2023 (Unfaithful CoT)

Dispatch 03 · Cognitive Science Lens: Predictive Coding, Sparse Coding & Superposition

Brains and Transformers: Where the Analogy Helps and Where It Breaks

The Core Idea in Plain Words Both biological cortex and artificial networks compress high-dimensional sensory streams into distributed representations and try to predict what comes next. Calling transformer dot-product attention "human attention", however, confuses a mathematical lookup operator with biological gain control.

In computational neuroscience, predictive processing models the visual cortex as a hierarchical circuit that constantly sends top-down predictions downward and passes only prediction errors upward (Rao & Ballard, 1999; Friston, 2010). Autoregressive language models share that predictive objective at the sequence level, and both systems rely on distributed codes: as Elhage et al. (2022) showed in Toy Models of Superposition, neural layers pack many more sparse features than they have neurons by exploiting almost-orthogonal directions.

Where the metaphor breaks down is just as important. Biological neurons communicate via metabolically constrained spikes in continuous time and adapt locally without a global backward pass. A transformer's context window is an exact, noiseless key-value buffer updated by global backpropagation across trillions of offline tokens. Neuroscience is a great source of mathematical hypotheses, not a license to claim biological equivalence.

Dispatch 04 · Collective Intelligence Lens: Directed Graphs, Message Passing & Aggregation

Multi-Agent Debate: Does Wiring Models Together Make Them Smarter?

The Core Idea in Plain Words Putting multiple LLM agents in a room sounds like an easy upgrade: one plans, one critiques, and one synthesizes. In graph-theoretic terms, however, more edges mean higher token cost and a real risk that confident wrong answers cascade across the network.

Papers like Du et al. (2023) and Liang et al. (2023) popularized multi-agent debate: having several model instances critique each other's drafts over multiple rounds to improve factuality and math accuracy. Mathematically, we can view an N-agent setup as a directed graph G = (V, E) where edges determine who reads whose message in round t, followed by a readout rule (majority vote, judge model, or sink node) that picks the final answer.

What makes this area fascinating is that communication is not a free lunch. An all-to-all clique scales message deliveries as O(N2) per round, rapidly inflating context length. Worse, if agents share the same pretraining priors, they often make correlated mistakes—and a persuasive hallucination can flip a lonely correct agent during peer revision. Designing multi-agent systems is really a problem in graph topology and aggregation rules, not just adding more personas.

Du et al. 2023 (Multi-Agent Debate) Liang et al. 2023 (Divergent Thinking via Debate)

Dispatch 05 · Grounding & Security Lens: Cosine Similarity, Vector Search & Adversarial Control

RAG and Indirect Prompt Injection: When Retrieved Data Turns Into Code

The Core Idea in Plain Words Retrieval-Augmented Generation fixes hallucination by fetching external passages via vector similarity. But because LLMs process instructions and retrieved data in the exact same token stream, a poisoned document can try to hijack the model's behaviour.

Instead of forcing a model to memorize every changing fact in its weights, Lewis et al. (2020) and Karpukhin et al. (2020) formulated Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR): embed queries and documents into a shared metric space, retrieve the top-k passages via inner-product or cosine similarity, and condition generation on that retrieved evidence.

As soon as RAG systems started browsing untrusted web pages, Greshake et al. (2023) highlighted a fundamental security flaw: indirect prompt injection. An attacker plants adversarial text inside an external document so that when the retriever pulls it into the context window, the payload tries to override the user's original prompt. Looking at the pipeline carefully reveals a two-stage bottleneck: first, whether a malicious passage survives retrieval and safety filtering to enter the top-k context; and second, whether the generator actually obeys the injected instruction once it is inside the prompt.

Dispatch 06 · Mechanistic Interpretability Lens: Kernel Similarity (CKA), Intrinsic Dimension & Causal Patching

Opening the Black Box: Geometry vs. Causal Circuits

The Core Idea in Plain Words Benchmark accuracy only tells you what a model outputs, not how. To look inside, researchers use two complementary toolkits: geometric measures that track how hidden point clouds deform across layers, and causal interventions that patch internal activations to prove which circuit actually drives a decision.

On the geometric side, tools like Centered Kernel Alignment (Kornblith et al., 2019) measure global Gram-matrix similarity between layers invariant to orthogonal rotations, while Two-Nearest-Neighbour (Facco et al., 2017) estimates the intrinsic dimension of the low-dimensional manifold that token embeddings trace inside ℝd. These metrics reveal how early layers expand features, middle layers reorganize local neighbourhoods, and late layers align directions with the output vocabulary.

Geometry alone, however, is observational—and correlation is not causation. To establish that an internal component actually implements an algorithm, mechanistic interpretability uses causal interventions: Olsson et al. (2022) isolated two-layer induction heads that copy patterns in-context (`[A][B] ... [A] -> [B]`), while Meng et al. (2022) used causal tracing (activation patching) to localize factual associations inside mid-layer MLP modules.

Dispatch 07 · AI for Science & Mathematics Lens: Partial Differential Equations, Evolutionary Search & Type Theory

Navier–Stokes, FunSearch, and Why Lean 4 Is the Ultimate Referee

The Core Idea in Plain Words Can an AI discover new mathematics or physics? Alone, an LLM is a brilliant but unreliable conjecture generator. Paired with numerical PDE solvers, evolutionary evaluators, or formal proof assistants like Lean 4, however, heuristic proposals get filtered by strict mathematical verification.

Look closely at the recent headlines in fluid dynamics and combinatorics. In fluid mechanics, Wang et al. (2025) used high-precision Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to discover new families of unstable self-similar blow-up solutions across fluid equations, while in early 2026 an OpenAI-assisted collaboration (OpenAI, 2026; 166-page proof PDF) established finite-time blow-up for an axisymmetric Navier–Stokes system with smooth external forcing. As SIAM News (2026) carefully noted, adding a C∞ forcing term is a major analytical achievement, but the classic unforced 3D Clay Millennium problem (f ≡ 0) remains wide open.

A clear pattern runs through every genuine breakthrough—including DeepMind's FunSearch on the cap-set problem, AlphaEvolve for algorithmic design, and AlphaProof pairing reinforcement learning with the Lean 4 theorem prover: the LLM proposes, and a deterministic mathematical verifier disposes. In formal type theory, a hallucinated lemma simply fails to type-check. That marriage of stochastic intuition and symbolic verification is where AI for mathematics becomes genuinely trustworthy.
